# Introduction to Measure Theory

Measure theory is the rigorous mathematical framework underpinning modern probability, integration, and much of analysis. It answers a deceptively simple question: *what does it mean to assign a size to a set?*

Before Lebesgue, integration was Riemann's domain — partitioning the *x*-axis and summing up rectangles. This works beautifully for continuous functions, but fails for objects like the Dirichlet function (rational indicator). Lebesgue's idea was to partition the *y*-axis instead, measuring the *size* of the preimage of each value. To do that, we first need a coherent notion of size — a **measure**.

## Sigma-Algebras

>[!definition] Measurable Space
> Let $X$ be a non-empty set. A collection $\mathcal{F} \subseteq 2^X$ is called a **$\sigma$-algebra** on $X$ if:
> 1. $X \in \mathcal{F}$
> 2. $A \in \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow A^c := X \setminus A \in \mathcal{F}$ *(closed under complements)*
> 3. $(A_n)_{n \geq 1} \subseteq \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ *(closed under countable unions)*
>
> The pair $(X, \mathcal{F})$ is called a **measurable space**, and elements of $\mathcal{F}$ are called **measurable sets**.

>[!remark]
> From these axioms, $\mathcal{F}$ is automatically closed under countable *intersections* (by de Morgan's law), finite unions, and set differences. The empty set $\emptyset = X^c$ also belongs to $\mathcal{F}$.

>[!example] Trivial examples
> - **Trivial $\sigma$-algebra**: $\mathcal{F} = \{\emptyset, X\}$ — the coarsest possible.
> - **Discrete $\sigma$-algebra**: $\mathcal{F} = 2^X$ — the finest possible, contains every subset.
> - **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$: generated by all open sets in $\mathbb{R}$. This is the standard $\sigma$-algebra on the real line.

The Borel $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is generated by all open intervals $(a, b)$, or equivalently by all half-lines $(-\infty, x]$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

>[!definition] Generated Sigma-Algebra
> For any collection $\mathcal{C} \subseteq 2^X$, the **$\sigma$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{C}$** is:
> $$\sigma(\mathcal{C}) := \bigcap \{ \mathcal{F} : \mathcal{F} \text{ is a } \sigma\text{-algebra and } \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{F} \}$$
> This is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$.

## Measures

>[!definition] Measure
> Let $(X, \mathcal{F})$ be a measurable space. A function $\mu : \mathcal{F} \to [0, +\infty]$ is a **measure** if:
> 1. $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$
> 2. **$\sigma$-additivity**: for any pairwise disjoint sequence $(A_n)_{n \geq 1} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$,
> $$\mu\!\left(\bigsqcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\right) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_n)$$
>
> The triple $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ is called a **measure space**.

>[!example] Canonical examples
> - **Lebesgue measure** $\lambda$ on $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))$: assigns to each interval its length, $\lambda([a,b]) = b - a$.
> - **Counting measure** on $(X, 2^X)$: $\mu(A) = |A|$ (number of elements, possibly $+\infty$).
> - **Dirac measure** at $x_0$: $\delta_{x_0}(A) = \mathbf{1}_{x_0 \in A}$.
> - **Probability measure**: a measure with $\mu(X) = 1$.

>[!theorem] Basic properties of measures
> Let $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu)$ be a measure space and $A, B, A_n \in \mathcal{F}$. Then:
> 1. **Monotonicity**: $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow \mu(A) \leq \mu(B)$
> 2. **Sub-additivity**: $\mu\!\left(\bigcup_n A_n\right) \leq \sum_n \mu(A_n)$
> 3. **Continuity from below**: $A_n \nearrow A \Rightarrow \mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)$
> 4. **Continuity from above**: $A_n \searrow A$ and $\mu(A_1) < \infty \Rightarrow \mu(A_n) \to \mu(A)$

>[!proof]- Proof of monotonicity
> If $A \subseteq B$, write $B = A \sqcup (B \setminus A)$. By $\sigma$-additivity (applied to the finite disjoint union):
> $$\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) \geq \mu(A)$$
> since $\mu(B \setminus A) \geq 0$. $\square$

>[!proof]- Proof of continuity from below
> Define $B_1 = A_1$ and $B_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n \geq 2$. Then $(B_n)$ are pairwise disjoint, $\bigsqcup_{k=1}^n B_k = A_n$, and $\bigsqcup_{k=1}^\infty B_k = A$. By $\sigma$-additivity:
> $$\mu(A) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \mu(B_k) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \mu(B_k) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(A_n). \quad \square$$

## Measurable Functions

>[!definition] Measurable Function
> Let $(X, \mathcal{F})$ and $(Y, \mathcal{G})$ be measurable spaces. A function $f: X \to Y$ is **$(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{G})$-measurable** if:
> $$\forall B \in \mathcal{G}, \quad f^{-1}(B) := \{x \in X : f(x) \in B\} \in \mathcal{F}$$
>
> When $Y = \mathbb{R}$ with the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, we simply say $f$ is **measurable** (or **Borel-measurable**).

>[!remark]
> It suffices to check measurability on a *generating* collection for $\mathcal{G}$. For Borel measurability on $\mathbb{R}$, it is enough to verify that $f^{-1}((-\infty, c]) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $c \in \mathbb{R}$.

>[!theorem] Stability under operations
> Let $f, g : X \to \mathbb{R}$ be measurable and $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Then the following are measurable:
> - $cf$, $f + g$, $fg$, $|f|$, $\max(f, g)$, $\min(f, g)$
> - $f/g$ (wherever $g \neq 0$)
> - $\sup_n f_n$ and $\limsup_n f_n$ for any sequence of measurable functions $(f_n)$

## The Lebesgue Integral

### Simple Functions

>[!definition] Simple Function
> A measurable function $\phi : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is **simple** if it takes finitely many values. It can always be written as:
> $$\phi = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \mathbf{1}_{A_k}$$
> with $a_k \geq 0$ and $A_k \in \mathcal{F}$ disjoint. Its integral is defined as:
> $$\int_X \phi \, d\mu := \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \mu(A_k) \in [0, +\infty]$$

### Non-Negative Functions

>[!definition] Lebesgue Integral (non-negative)
> For $f : X \to [0, +\infty]$ measurable, the **Lebesgue integral** is:
> $$\int_X f \, d\mu := \sup \left\{ \int_X \phi \, d\mu : \phi \text{ simple}, \; 0 \leq \phi \leq f \right\}$$

### Convergence Theorems

The power of Lebesgue integration lies in its convergence theorems.

>[!theorem] Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT)
> Let $0 \leq f_1 \leq f_2 \leq \cdots$ be an increasing sequence of non-negative measurable functions with pointwise limit $f = \lim_n f_n$. Then:
> $$\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu$$

>[!theorem] Fatou's Lemma
> For any sequence $(f_n)$ of non-negative measurable functions:
> $$\int_X \liminf_{n \to \infty} f_n \, d\mu \leq \liminf_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu$$

>[!theorem] Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT)
> Let $(f_n)$ be measurable functions converging pointwise to $f$, with $|f_n| \leq g$ for all $n$ where $g \in L^1(\mu)$ (i.e. $\int g \, d\mu < \infty$). Then $f \in L^1(\mu)$ and:
> $$\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu$$

>[!remark]
> The condition $|f_n| \leq g$ (the **dominating function**) is essential. Without it the result fails: consider $f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with Lebesgue measure — each integral equals 1, $f_n \to 0$ pointwise, yet $\int f = 0$.

>[!intuition]- Why does the DCT work?
> Intuitively: if the functions are all bounded by an integrable envelope $g$, then the mass cannot "escape to infinity" or concentrate on a negligible set. The domination condition is what prevents pathological behaviour like the sequence above.

## The $L^p$ Spaces

>[!definition] $L^p$ space
> For $1 \leq p < \infty$, the space $L^p(X, \mu)$ consists of (equivalence classes of) measurable functions with finite $p$-norm:
> $$\|f\|_p := \left( \int_X |f|^p \, d\mu \right)^{1/p} < \infty$$
> For $p = \infty$: $\|f\|_\infty := \operatorname{ess\,sup} |f| < \infty$ (essential supremum).

>[!theorem] Hölder's Inequality
> For $p, q \geq 1$ with $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = 1$ (conjugate exponents), and $f \in L^p$, $g \in L^q$:
> $$\int_X |fg| \, d\mu \leq \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$$

>[!corollary] Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
> Taking $p = q = 2$: for $f, g \in L^2$,
> $$\int_X |fg| \, d\mu \leq \|f\|_2 \|g\|_2$$

>[!theorem] Minkowski's Inequality
> For $1 \leq p \leq \infty$ and $f, g \in L^p$:
> $$\|f + g\|_p \leq \|f\|_p + \|g\|_p$$
> This confirms that $L^p$ is a **Banach space** (a complete normed vector space).

## The Carathéodory Extension Theorem

A fundamental question: given a function defined only on a simple collection of sets (e.g. intervals), can we extend it to a full measure on the generated $\sigma$-algebra?

>[!definition] Outer Measure
> A function $\mu^* : 2^X \to [0, +\infty]$ is an **outer measure** if:
> 1. $\mu^*(\emptyset) = 0$
> 2. $A \subseteq B \Rightarrow \mu^*(A) \leq \mu^*(B)$
> 3. $\mu^*\!\left(\bigcup_n A_n\right) \leq \sum_n \mu^*(A_n)$ (countable sub-additivity)

>[!theorem] Carathéodory's Extension Theorem
> Let $\mu_0$ be a **pre-measure** on an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ on $X$. Define the outer measure:
> $$\mu^*(A) := \inf \left\{ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \mu_0(A_n) : A_n \in \mathcal{A},\; A \subseteq \bigcup_n A_n \right\}$$
> Then $\mu^*$ restricts to a measure $\mu$ on $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$, extending $\mu_0$. If $\mu_0$ is $\sigma$-finite, the extension is unique.

>[!example] Lebesgue measure via Carathéodory
> Start with $\mathcal{A}$ = finite unions of intervals in $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mu_0((a,b]) = b - a$. Carathéodory's theorem produces the Lebesgue measure on $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ — rigorously justifying that length extends to a measure on all Borel sets.

## Summary

| Concept | Definition |
|---------|-----------|
| $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ | Collection of subsets closed under complements and countable unions |
| Measure $\mu$ | $[0,\infty]$-valued $\sigma$-additive function with $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ |
| Measurable function | Preimages of Borel sets are measurable |
| Lebesgue integral | Supremum of integrals of simple approximations |
| $L^p$ space | Functions with finite $p$-th moment |

>[!note] Connection to Probability
> A probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is simply a measure space with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$. Random variables are measurable functions, expectations are Lebesgue integrals, and the DCT justifies swapping limits and expectations — a tool used constantly in stochastic analysis.
